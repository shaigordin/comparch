Module 9: Case Study II — Bronze Age Axes and Craft Networks

Week 9 — Outlines and the Bronze Age

Archaeological Background

The European Bronze Age and Its Metal

The Bronze Age in Europe (c. 2300–800 BCE) was defined by metallurgy. Bronze — an alloy of copper and tin — required long-distance exchange networks, because copper and tin deposits are rarely found together. The very existence of Bronze Age material culture implies connectivity.

Flanged axes are among the most common Bronze Age metal finds. They were functional tools (woodworking, combat) but also prestige objects, hoarded in deposits, and possibly used as currency-like valuables.

The “flanged” design — raised ridges along the sides to help hafting — was a technological solution that spread across Europe in the Middle Bronze Age. But how did this spread happen?

G3 and G4 Groups

Our dataset comes from Central European flanged axes classified into two typological groups (designations following standard Bronze Age typo-chronology):

  • G3: Earlier group, c. 1600–1400 BCE. Primarily found in western Central Europe.
  • G4: Later group, c. 1400–1200 BCE. Wider geographic distribution.

Traditional typology classifies them by a combination of proportions (flange height, blade width relative to total length) and decorative features. But typology is always a simplification.

The question: does shape variation in these axes respect the G3/G4 boundary? Is there a continuous morphological gradient? Are there regional variants within each group?

Why This Matters Historically

Three possible outcomes:

Outcome A: Clean separation

G3 and G4 form distinct, non-overlapping clusters in shape space. This supports the idea that each group represents a coherent production tradition — perhaps a specific workshop, region, or period with its own design template.

Implication: Bronze Age metalworkers maintained strict shape standards within each tradition, possibly through apprenticeship networks with limited cross-transmission.

Outcome B: Overlapping clusters

G3 and G4 overlap substantially in shape space. Shape variation is continuous, not categorical.

Implication: The G3/G4 typological boundary may be a modern analytical construct rather than an emic (insider) category recognized by Bronze Age communities. Shape changed gradually, not in a discrete step.

Outcome C: Geographic gradient cuts across typological groups

PC axes that capture most variation correlate with geographic position, not with G3/G4 group membership.

Implication: Shape variation reflects local production traditions that don’t align with the typological sequence — regional craft identity was stronger than temporal change.

None of these outcomes is “right” or “wrong.” Each tells us something important about Bronze Age social and technological organization.

The Statistical Test

We use a permutation-based MANOVA (the same logic as for coins, applied to EFA+PCA scores):

# Permutation MANOVA on EFA PCA scores
f_stat, p_val = permutation_manova(scores, labels, n_perm=999, n_pcs=8)

print(f"\nPermutation MANOVA (G3 vs G4):")
print(f"  F = {f_stat:.3f}")
print(f"  p = {p_val:.3f}")
print(f"  {'SIGNIFICANT' if p_val < 0.05 else 'NOT SIGNIFICANT'} at α = 0.05")

# Effect size: proportion of variance explained
r2 = f_stat / (f_stat + (len(labels) - 2))
print(f"  R² ≈ {r2:.3f} ({r2*100:.1f}% of variance explained by group)")

Interpreting Effect Size

R² value Interpretation
< 0.05 Very small effect — groups barely differ
0.05–0.15 Small effect
0.15–0.35 Medium effect
> 0.35 Large effect — groups strongly differ

Even if the test is significant (p < 0.05), a small R² means groups differ reliably but not dramatically.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import matplotlib.cm as cm
import matplotlib.colors as mcolors

# Load geographic metadata
geo = pd.read_csv("data/flanged_axes/axes_metadata.csv")
# Columns: name, group, latitude, longitude, country, site_name

# Merge with PCA scores
df_geo = pd.merge(df, geo, on='name')

# Map PC1 score to color (blue = negative, red = positive)
norm = mcolors.TwoSlopeNorm(vcenter=0,
                              vmin=df_geo['PC1'].min(),
                              vmax=df_geo['PC1'].max())
colors_map = cm.RdBu_r(norm(df_geo['PC1']))

# Simple scatter map (approximate)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 8))
sc = ax.scatter(df_geo['longitude'], df_geo['latitude'],
                c=df_geo['PC1'], cmap='RdBu_r', s=80,
                edgecolor='black', linewidth=0.5, norm=norm)

# Label markers with group
for _, row in df_geo.iterrows():
    ax.annotate(row['group'], (row['longitude'], row['latitude']),
                fontsize=6, ha='center', va='bottom')

plt.colorbar(sc, label='PC1 score (shape axis)')
ax.set_xlabel("Longitude")
ax.set_ylabel("Latitude")
ax.set_title("Geographic distribution of PC1 shape scores — Flanged Axes")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
