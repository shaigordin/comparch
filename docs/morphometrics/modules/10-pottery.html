Module 10: Case Study III — Pottery and Ceramic Traditions

Week 10 — From Typology to Shape Space

Why Pottery?

Pottery is the most ubiquitous artefact in archaeological sites almost everywhere in the world. It survives well, is highly sensitive to cultural traditions, and has been classified typologically for over a century. It is also the ideal object for outline analysis: vessel profiles are smooth curves with no discrete landmark points.

Pottery shape reflects: - Production technique — wheel-thrown vs. hand-built produces different profile shapes - Function — storage jars, cooking pots, and serving bowls have characteristic morphologies - Chronology — vessel forms changed over time in most traditions - Cultural identity — potters learned their craft within communities; shape encodes that transmission - Exchange — when pottery moved between communities, shapes were copied (and subtly transformed)

This week has two parts. First you read about a published case study. Then you run your own analysis on a real open-access dataset.

Part I — Published Case Study: Bibracte Celtic Ceramics

What was the question?

Ceramic typology is built on the expert’s eye: an archaeologist looks at a vessel and assigns it to a type. But can a computer reach the same conclusion using shape alone? And if so, what does this tell us about whether typology captures real morphological structure?

This is the question that Wilczek et al. (2014) asked using 154 complete Iron Age vessels from Bibracte, a major Celtic oppidum (fortified town) in Burgundy, France. Bibracte was the capital of the Aedui tribe in the Second Iron Age (2nd century BC – 1st century AD) and is one of the most extensively excavated oppida in Europe.

The dataset

154 vessels with complete, well-preserved cross-sections. Assigned to 8 functional types by the traditional typology of Barral et al. (1995):

Type N Character
Plates 35 Wide and flat; open form
Dishes 20 Shallower than plates
Bowls 24 Hemispherical; open
Cups 18 Small, closed drinking vessels
Goblets 14 Tall, narrow; drinking
Pots 27 Medium, closed; cooking/storage
Vases 9 Wide body; storage
Bottles 7 Narrow neck; closed

Outlines were extracted from published archaeological drawings (1200 dpi scans, external half cross-section mirrored to full profile), then processed by Elliptic Fourier Analysis (EFA) using 20 harmonics.

What they found

Key result (Wilczek et al. 2014)

PC1 captures ~93% of total shape variance. The main axis of shape variation is elongation: plates occupy one extreme (wide, flat) and bottles occupy the other (tall, narrow neck). Bowls, pots, cups, and goblets fall between these endpoints.

An unsupervised computer clustering of shape space (with no knowledge of the traditional types) produced 7 clusters that closely match the 8 traditional types — the computer and the archaeologist largely agreed. This validates that EFA captures the same morphological structure that typologists have described intuitively for decades.

Importantly, some types show high internal shape variation (pots, goblets) while others are tightly clustered (plates, bottles). This reveals which types are morphologically coherent and which may overlap functionally.

Why this matters

EFA makes typology reproducible and quantifiable. Anyone can run the same code on the same images and get the same result. The expert is still needed to interpret those results archaeologically — but the initial classification can be automated and tested statistically.

Citation: Wilczek, J., Monna, F., Barral, P., Burlet, L., Chateau, C., Navarro, N. (2014). Morphometrics of Second Iron Age ceramics — strengths, weaknesses, and comparison with traditional typology. Journal of Archaeological Science 50: 39–50. DOI: 10.1016/j.jas.2014.05.033

Part II — Your Analysis: Ogame Jars from Edo-Period Japan

The research question

Not all shape questions are about typology. A different and equally important question is about production and selection:

Are jars from kiln sites (where they were made) more standardized in shape than jars from burial contexts (where families chose specific vessels to inter with the dead)?

If kiln-produced jars are highly uniform, that tells us something about production organization — potters working to a standard. If burial jars show different or more varied shapes, that might mean families selected visually distinctive vessels, or that different kilns supplied different markets.

This question is answerable with EFA + PCA.

The dataset

Loftus, J. (2025). A Geometric Morphometric Dataset of Edo-Period ‘Ogame’ Jar Profiles from Northern Kyushu, Japan. Journal of Open Archaeology Data 13:13. DOI: 10.5334/joad.159 | Data: zenodo.org/records/15781166

Ogame (大甕, “large jars”) are thick-walled stoneware vessels produced in the Hizen and Chikuzen provinces of northern Kyushu during the Edo period (c. AD 1600–1800). They served diverse functions: storing soy sauce and other goods, and also as burial containers for human remains.

Specimens 243 jars
Sites 12 (2 kiln sites + 9 mortuary sites + 1 museum collection)
Format Standardized PNG silhouette images, one per jar

MR Maruya Mortuary 36
NT Nishitakagi Mortuary 21
NS Narasaki Mortuary 11
MD Mukaida Mortuary 12
MT Mitsutake Mortuary 10
HK Hakozaki Mortuary 4
KS, KT, TJ, TK Other mortuary Mortuary 12

Step 1 — Install and import

import os, zipfile, requests
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from PIL import Image
from skimage import measure
import pyefd
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import mannwhitneyu

Step 2 — Download and unpack the dataset

# Download from Zenodo (run once — ~30 MB)
url = "https://zenodo.org/records/15781166/files/Ogame_GMM.zip?download=1"
r = requests.get(url, stream=True)
with open("Ogame_GMM.zip", "wb") as f:
    for chunk in r.iter_content(chunk_size=8192):
        f.write(chunk)

with zipfile.ZipFile("Ogame_GMM.zip") as z:
    z.extractall(".")
print("Done. Files extracted to Ogame_GMM/")

Step 3 — Load metadata

samples = pd.read_csv("Ogame_GMM/Ogame_Samples.csv")
# Rename the long column for convenience
samples = samples.rename(columns={
    samples.columns[3]: "context_code"
})
samples["context"] = samples["context_code"].map({1: "Burial", 2: "Kiln"})
print(samples["context"].value_counts())
print(samples["Site Code"].value_counts())

Step 4 — Extract outlines and compute EFA

def extract_outline(image_path):
    """Extract the external outline of a black vessel silhouette on white background."""
    img = np.array(Image.open(image_path).convert('L'))
    binary = img < 128                         # True where vessel is (dark pixels)
    contours = measure.find_contours(binary.astype(float), 0.5)
    if not contours:
        return None
    return max(contours, key=len)              # largest contour = vessel outline

records = []
img_dir = "Ogame_GMM/Ogame_Profiles_All"

for _, row in samples.iterrows():
    path = os.path.join(img_dir, f"{row['Sample ID']}.png")
    if not os.path.exists(path):
        continue
    outline = extract_outline(path)
    if outline is None or len(outline) < 50:
        continue
    y, x = outline[:, 0], outline[:, 1]   # skimage returns (row, col)
    # Compute EFA with 20 harmonics, normalised for size and orientation
    coeffs = pyefd.elliptic_fourier_descriptors(
        np.column_stack([x, y]), order=20, normalize=True
    )
    records.append({
        "sample_id":  row["Sample ID"],
        "site_code":  row["Site Code"],
        "context":    row["context"],
        **{f"c{i:03d}": v for i, v in enumerate(coeffs.ravel())}
    })

df = pd.DataFrame(records)
print(f"Processed {len(df)} jars successfully")

Step 5 — PCA and visualisation

coeff_cols = [c for c in df.columns if c.startswith("c")]
X = df[coeff_cols].values

pca = PCA()
scores = pca.fit_transform(X)
var1 = pca.explained_variance_ratio_[0] * 100
var2 = pca.explained_variance_ratio_[1] * 100

# ── Plot 1: colour by depositional context ───────────────────────────────────
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(14, 6))

colors = {"Kiln": "#e07b39", "Burial": "#4a90c4"}
for ctx, col in colors.items():
    mask = df["context"] == ctx
    axes[0].scatter(scores[mask, 0], scores[mask, 1],
                    c=col, label=f"{ctx} (n={mask.sum()})",
                    s=50, alpha=0.75, edgecolors="white", linewidths=0.5)

axes[0].set_xlabel(f"PC1 ({var1:.1f}%)")
axes[0].set_ylabel(f"PC2 ({var2:.1f}%)")
axes[0].set_title("Ogame Jar Shape Space — by Depositional Context")
axes[0].axhline(0, color="grey", lw=0.5, ls="--")
axes[0].axvline(0, color="grey", lw=0.5, ls="--")
axes[0].legend()

# ── Plot 2: colour by production site (kiln sites only) ──────────────────────
kiln_mask = df["context"] == "Kiln"
kiln_sites = df.loc[kiln_mask, "site_code"].unique()
kiln_colors = {"FR": "#d62728", "KM": "#ff7f0e"}
for site in kiln_sites:
    mask = df["site_code"] == site
    axes[1].scatter(scores[mask, 0], scores[mask, 1],
                    c=kiln_colors.get(site, "#aaaaaa"),
                    label=f"{site} (n={mask.sum()})",
                    s=55, alpha=0.8, edgecolors="white", linewidths=0.5)

axes[1].set_xlabel(f"PC1 ({var1:.1f}%)")
axes[1].set_ylabel(f"PC2 ({var2:.1f}%)")
axes[1].set_title("Kiln Sites Compared — FR vs KM")
axes[1].axhline(0, color="grey", lw=0.5, ls="--")
axes[1].axvline(0, color="grey", lw=0.5, ls="--")
axes[1].legend()

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("ogame_pca.png", dpi=150, bbox_inches="tight")
plt.show()
print(f"PC1: {var1:.1f}% | PC2: {var2:.1f}% | Together: {var1+var2:.1f}%")

Step 6 — Statistical test (kiln vs. burial)

pc1_kiln   = scores[df["context"] == "Kiln",   0]
pc1_burial = scores[df["context"] == "Burial",  0]

stat, p = mannwhitneyu(pc1_kiln, pc1_burial, alternative="two-sided")
print(f"Mann-Whitney U test on PC1 scores:")
print(f"  Kiln mean PC1:   {pc1_kiln.mean():.4f}")
print(f"  Burial mean PC1: {pc1_burial.mean():.4f}")
print(f"  U = {stat:.1f}, p = {p:.4f}")
if p < 0.05:
    print("  → Significant difference in shape between kiln and burial jars.")
else:
    print("  → No significant difference — shapes overlap.")

Interpreting the Results

Work through these questions with your PCA plots:

  1. Do kiln jars and burial jars separate in shape space? If yes, what does that mean archaeologically? If no — what might explain the lack of difference?

  2. Do the two kiln sites (FR and KM) cluster separately? If yes, this suggests each workshop had its own production style. If they overlap, kilns were producing to a shared regional standard.

  3. What shape features drive PC1? Look at jars plotted at the extreme left vs. extreme right of your scatter plot. Ask: what is visually different about those vessels?

  4. What is the spread within each context? High variance in burial jars could mean families chose idiosyncratic vessels; low variance in kiln jars would suggest production standardization.

  5. Connect to historical context: The Edo period (1600–1800) was a time of expanding commercial production in Kyushu. Large kilns like Furukameyashimo (FR) produced for regional markets. Did commercial production lead to more standardized jar forms than jars selected for burial?

```python
print(f"  U = {stat:.1f}, p = {p:.4f}")
if p < 0.05:
    print("  → Significant difference in shape between kiln and burial jars.")
else:
    print("  → No significant difference — shapes overlap.")
```
