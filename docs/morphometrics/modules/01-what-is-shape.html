Module 1: What Is Shape? Why Does It Matter?

Week 1 — Foundations

The Central Question

Every object ever made by human hands has a shape. Archaeologists have always described shape — “biconical,” “carinated,” “leaf-shaped” — but what if we could measure it? What if we could compare the shapes of hundreds of objects objectively, statistically, and reproducibly?

That is what geometric morphometrics (GM) does.

Size vs. Shape: The Fundamental Distinction

The most important concept in this course is the difference between size and shape.

Kendall’s Definition (1977)

Shape is “all the geometric information that remains when location, scale and rotation effects are filtered out from an object.”

In other words: shape is what’s left when you remove where it is, how big it is, and which way it’s pointing.

Think of a wine bottle. Two bottles can have the same shape but be different sizes (a regular bottle vs. a magnum). Two bottles can be the same size but different shapes (Bordeaux vs. Burgundy style). We are interested in shape — independent of size.

For archaeology, this distinction is fundamental: - A Roman denarius from mint A and mint B might weigh the same (same size) but have subtly different portrait proportions (different shape) - Two Bronze Age axes from different regions might be the same length (same size) but have different blade geometries (different shape)

What Shape Can Tell Us

Shape in archaeological objects is not accidental. It encodes:

Signal Example
Technological tradition Bronze Age axes made by the same tradition share outline shape
Chronological change Coin portrait conventions evolved over time — shape records this
Regional identity Pottery production traditions leave shape signatures
Function Storage jars have different shape profiles than cooking pots
Exchange and copying Imitated objects share shape with their originals
Standardization Mass-produced objects (coins) show low shape variation when quality control was high

Three Case Studies We Will Explore

1. Roman Imperial Coins

Roman denarii of Hadrian (r. 117–138 CE) and Antoninus Pius (r. 138–161 CE).

The question: did Roman die-cutters maintain consistent shape standards? Can we detect differences between emperors’ coinages from shape alone?

The Roman Empire produced coins in enormous quantities. Die-cutters were skilled artisans working under imperial oversight. Every coin was intended to look the same — but did they? And if there are subtle shape differences, what do they mean?

2. European Bronze Age Flanged Axes

Bronze Age metalworkers cast flanged axes according to visual templates — there were no written instructions, no blueprints. Shape was transmitted through apprenticeship and observation.

The dataset includes two typological groups (G3 and G4) from across Central Europe. The question: do these typological groups reflect genuine morphological distinctions, or is shape variation continuous? And can we detect regional craft traditions from shape alone?

3. Ancient Pottery

Pottery is the most common archaeological find almost everywhere in the world. Its shape reflects the potter’s training, the community’s traditions, the vessel’s function, and the period of production.

The pottery dataset (published open-access 2023–2025) will let us ask: can we identify production traditions or chronological periods purely from outline shape?

Traditional Morphometrics vs. Geometric Morphometrics

Before GM, archaeologists measured objects with calipers: length, width, height, diameter. These measurements are called traditional morphometrics or multivariate morphometrics.

The problem: measurements depend on where you put the caliper. They don’t capture shape — they capture specific distances that may or may not represent the shape differences you care about.

GM solves this by working directly with coordinates — the \((x, y)\) positions of specific points on the object. This captures the full geometry of the shape, not just a few distances.

Traditional Morphometrics Geometric Morphometrics
Distances and ratios Coordinates of points
Loses geometric information Preserves full geometry
Hard to visualize Visualization via deformation grids
No standard alignment Procrustes alignment removes size/position/rotation
Many variables, unclear meaning Shape as a single multivariate variable

The Three Methods We Will Use

1. Landmark Analysis

Place discrete points — landmarks — at biologically or archaeologically meaningful locations on the object. This is the main method for the coin analysis.

2. Outline Analysis (Elliptic Fourier Analysis)

Trace the outline (contour) of the object and decompose it into mathematical functions. This is the main method for the flanged axes and pottery.

3. 3D Morphometrics (Week 11 overview)

Extend landmarks and surfaces into three dimensions using photogrammetry or scanning. We will not do a full 3D analysis but will understand the principles.

Discussion Questions

  1. Think of an artefact type you find interesting. What shape features would you want to capture? Would you use landmarks or outlines?

  2. The slides from our source course show a coin, a vessel, and a bronze axe, and list their attributes. Which attributes are size and which are shape?

  3. “Shape is all the geometric information remaining after location, scale, and rotation are removed.” — Can you think of any information about a shape that this definition misses?

Looking Ahead
