Final Project

50% of final grade | Written report due one week after Week 12

Overview

The final project is your opportunity to apply everything you have learned to a shape question of your own choosing. It culminates the course’s progressive build from individual assignments into an original, small-scale research investigation.

Choose Your Track

Track A: Extended Dataset Analysis (recommended for most students)

Take one of the three course datasets — coins, axes, or pottery — and go deeper than the corresponding assignment. Extensions could include:

  • Testing additional grouping variables from the metadata (chronological phase, geographic region, deposition context)
  • Allometric analysis: does shape change with size?
  • Comparing results of two methods on the same objects (e.g., landmarks vs. outlines on the coins)
  • Comparing your results to a published analysis of the same or similar material

Why this track: You already know the data. You can focus on asking a better question rather than learning a new dataset.

Track B: New Open-Access Dataset

Find an open-access GM dataset from a published paper (2020–2025) on an artefact type not covered in the course. Replicate or extend the published analysis.

Where to find datasets: - Zenodo.org — search “geometric morphometrics archaeology” - OSF (Open Science Framework) - Figshare - Journal supplementary data: Journal of Archaeological Science, Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports, Archaeological and Anthropological Sciences

What counts as a valid dataset: - Open-access coordinates (TPS, CSV, or similar) from a peer-reviewed paper - Minimum 20 specimens - At least one grouping variable (period, region, type, etc.)

What to submit along with your report: Full citation of the paper you are using, DOI of the dataset, and confirmation that it is genuinely open-access.

Track C: Research Proposal (writing-only option)

For students who prefer writing to data analysis: write a detailed research proposal for a GM study on an artefact type of your choice.

Required sections: 1. Introduction — what is the archaeological problem? 2. Research question — specific and answerable 3. Dataset design — what objects, from where, how many, in which collections? 4. Methodology — which GM method, how many landmarks or which outline, what software? 5. Expected results — what do you predict and why? 6. Possible complications — what could go wrong, and how would you handle it? 7. Significance — what would a positive result tell us about the past? 8. References — minimum 10 peer-reviewed sources

Length: 3000 words (excluding references)

Report Requirements (Tracks A and B)

Length: 2000–3000 words (excluding figures and references)

Structure: 1. Introduction — the archaeological problem and why shape matters 2. Research Question — stated explicitly and concisely 3. Data and Methods — dataset description, GM method, statistical approach 4. Results — present figures and statistics, no interpretation yet 5. Discussion — interpret results in archaeological terms; compare to existing literature 6. Conclusion — main finding in 3–4 sentences; one future direction 7. References — minimum 8 peer-reviewed sources (APA or Chicago author-date)

Figures: Minimum 3: - A PCA scatter plot (labeled, with group color-coding) - A shape visualization (deformation grid or reconstructed EFA shapes) - One additional figure of your choice (allometry plot, map, dendrogram, etc.)

Code: Submit your Colab notebook link as an appendix. It must be set to “Anyone with link can view.”

Presentation (Week 12)

Format: 8 minutes + 2 minutes Q&A

What to cover: 1. Research question (1 min) 2. Dataset and method (1 min) 3. Your key result — your best figure (3 min) 4. Archaeological interpretation (2 min) 5. One limitation and one future direction (1 min)

Slides: Optional but recommended. Maximum 8 slides.

Grading Rubric (Tracks A and B)

Criterion Points
Research question — clarity, originality, archaeological relevance 20
Analysis — correct and complete GM analysis with appropriate statistics 25
Figures — quality, clarity, appropriate labeling 20
Interpretation — connects shape data to archaeological question; engages with literature 25
Writing — clear, well-organized, properly referenced 10
Total 100

Grading Rubric (Track C — Proposal)

Criterion Points
Research question — specific and answerable 20
Dataset design — realistic and well-justified 20
Methodology — correct and appropriate 25
Critical reflection — complications and limitations addressed 20
Writing quality and referencing 15
Total 100

Final Project Timeline

Date Milestone
Week 7 Choose your track and topic — notify instructor
Week 9 Brief check-in (5 min per student) — confirm dataset and question
Week 11 Draft results (figures) ready for in-class feedback
Week 12 In-class presentations
One week later Written report due

Tips for Success

  1. Start with a clear, answerable question. The most common mistake is a question too vague to test with shape data. “I will analyze pottery shape” is not a question. “Do pottery vessels from Site A differ in outline shape from vessels of the same period at Site B, and if so, does the difference suggest distinct production communities?” is a question.

  2. Don’t over-analyze. A clear, well-interpreted result with one PCA and one statistical test is better than five tests you can’t explain.

  3. Engage with the existing literature. What does the paper that published your dataset say? What can you add, confirm, or contest?

  4. Let AI write the code, but don’t let AI write the text. Your interpretation is the entire point.
